Abstract

A large part of India’s workforce learned its trade informally and holds no certificate. The Recognition of Prior Learning (RPL) route under the National Skills Qualification Framework (NSQF) can certify such workers, but in practice it depends on manual practical assessment, which is slow to scale and inconsistent between assessors. We present Hunar, an assessor-support tool for the domestic electrician trade. Hunar (i) maps a worker’s free-text self-declaration in English, Hindi or Hinglish to the closest NSQF qualification packs, using performance criteria parsed from the official qualification files on the National Qualification Register, and explains each match; (ii) guides a practical assessment with six task rubrics whose anchored 0 to 3 levels are linked to official National Occupational Standards; (iii) computes explainable pre scores from the assessor’s checklist observations and photo evidence, which the assessor accepts or overrides with a reason; (iv) checks evidence photos for reuse with perceptual hashing and, once trained, for the visible presence of protective equipment; (v) produces an NSQF-aligned competency profile and a recommendation that only an assessor’s sign-off can finalise, with every action in a hash-chained audit log; and (vi) works offline through an ordered, idempotent sync queue.

On a synthetic set of 150 declarations (pipeline check only), keyword and TF-IDF baselines fall to near-chance top-1 accuracy on Hindi in Devanagari (0.25 for TF-IDF; chance 0.20), while a multilingual MiniLM model reaches 0.42. An exploratory hybrid of the two reaches 0.92 top-3 accuracy overall. On real photographs from a public PPE dataset with simulated reuse, perceptual hashing caught every recompressed or resized copy at a false-alarm rate of 0.016 on unrelated photos, but missed cropped, rotated and bordered copies. The central claim of the problem statement, improved agreement between assessors, needs a study with real assessors; Hunar includes the rater mode and analysis for it, and that result is pending.

1. Introduction

1.1 Background

India’s official labour statistics show how much of the workforce is outside formal employment. The Periodic Labour Force Survey 2025 reports that 73.1% of usual-status workers in the non-agricultural sector work in proprietary and partnership enterprises, which the survey treats as the informal sector [11]. Many of these workers, electricians among them, learned their trade on the job or from family members and have no certificate to show for it.

The National Skills Qualification Framework (NSQF) organises qualifications by level of competence [1], [2], and the National Council for Vocational Education and Training (NCVET), constituted in 2018 [3], regulates them. Approved qualifications and their National Occupational Standards (NOS) are published on the National Qualification Register (NQR) [7]. Recognition of Prior Learning lets an experienced worker be assessed and certified against these standards without repeating training [4], [8], [9], [10]; RPL has also been a component of the Pradhan Mantri Kaushal Vikas Yojana (PMKVY) [6]. Evidence from an RPL pilot in four Indian sectors shows both the value of RPL and the practical difficulty of running it at scale [60], [61].

1.2 Motivation

The SIH26242 statement identifies the bottleneck: RPL assessment relies on manual practical evaluation, which is slow to scale, inconsistent between assessors and locations, and hard to schedule. It asks for a tool that guides the worker’s self-declaration, maps it to qualification packs, supports practical evaluation with checklists and image aids, standardises rubrics to reduce variance between evaluators, generates an NSQF-aligned competency profile for assessor sign-off, and works in low-connectivity settings. It also asks for a clear statement of where the tool supports and where it would replace human judgement.

1.3 Objectives and scope

  • Build a working end-to-end flow for one trade, domestic electrician, using real qualification files from the NQR: the Assistant Electrician qualification (CSDCI, NSQF level 3) as the target and four neighbouring electrical qualifications as distractors.
  • Support English, Hindi (Devanagari) and Hinglish declarations and an English/Hindi interface.
  • Run on an ordinary student laptop on CPU; GPU training on Google Colab.
  • Keep the assessor in charge: the tool suggests, the assessor decides, and every decision is traceable.

1.4 Contributions

  1. A parser that turns NCVET qualification-file PDFs into structured JSON (5 qualifications, 27 NOS, 344 performance criteria), with source URL, download date and SHA-256.
  2. A criterion-level, explainable qualification-pack mapper with keyword and TF-IDF baselines, two multilingual embedding models, and a per-language (fairness) evaluation.
  3. Six practical-task rubrics linked to official NOS and performance criteria, and a rubric engine with transparent pre-score rules, tested aggregation maths, the official pass mark and a safety-critical blocking rule.
  4. An evidence-integrity evaluation of perceptual hashing on real photographs.
  5. An offline-first application with an append-only, hash-chained audit trail.
  6. A ready-to-run protocol, rater mode and analysis for measuring inter-assessor agreement.

2. Literature review

RPL policy and practice. UNESCO’s guidelines describe recognition, validation and accreditation of non-formal and informal learning as a route to lifelong learning [8]. ILO work identifies the success factors of RPL systems, including credible assessment, trained assessors and quality assurance [9], [10]. India’s RPL guidelines define the process under NCVET [4], aligned to the NSQF [2] and the National Credit Framework [5]. Rothboeck et al. study an Indian RPL pilot across four sectors [60], and Singh and Ehlers compare RPL policy in Denmark and India [61]. Rambharose surveys the role of artificial intelligence in RPL and the opportunities it opens for adult assessment [62]. To our knowledge there is little published work that evaluates an AI tool inside an Indian RPL assessment; this is the gap Hunar addresses at prototype scale.

Matching text to skills and occupations. Taxonomy-driven job matching is well established in Europe through ESCO [18]. Skill extraction from job postings has been studied with annotated corpora such as SkillSpan [19], with representation learning for job titles [20], with multilingual taxonomy-driven pre-training [22], and is surveyed in [21]. These works match job postings to a taxonomy; Hunar instead matches a worker’s own description of their experience, written informally and often in Hindi or Hinglish, to the performance criteria of Indian qualification packs.

Sentence embeddings. BERT [17] enabled contextual representations; Sentence-BERT made them efficient for semantic similarity [13]. Reimers and Gurevych extended sentence embeddings to many languages by knowledge distillation [14]; the paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2 model we use comes from that work and is built on the compact MiniLM architecture [16]. LaBSE is a language-agnostic alternative trained for translation retrieval [15]. Lexical baselines are TF-IDF weighting [23] and BM25 [24]; mean reciprocal rank comes from question-answering evaluation [25].

Code-mixed Hindi-English. Hinglish is common in informal writing [27] and remains hard for NLP models [26], [28]. Our per-language evaluation therefore treats language as a fairness dimension: a tool that works only in English would exclude the workers RPL is meant for.

Visual evidence. YOLO introduced single-stage real-time detection [29]; Ultralytics YOLOv8 is a widely used implementation [30]; mAP follows the COCO protocol [31]. PPE detection on construction sites has been studied with deep learning [32], [33], [34]; Wang et al. also report that detection accuracy drops when faces are blurred [34], which matters for Hunar because it blurs faces for privacy. Face detection for blurring builds on Viola and Jones [58], and large-scale automatic face blurring was deployed in Street View [59].

Evidence integrity. Perceptual hashes map visually similar images to nearby codes [35], [36], [37]; Farid gives a recent overview of their strengths and failure modes [38]. We use the ImageHash library [39].

Assessor consistency. Agreement beyond chance is measured with Cohen’s kappa [40], Fleiss’ kappa for many raters [41] and Krippendorff’s alpha, which handles ordinal data and missing ratings [43], [44]; interpretation conventions [42] and tutorials [45] are widely used, and bootstrap confidence intervals [46] suit small samples. Scoring rubrics can improve reliability when they are analytic, topic-specific and come with anchors and rater training [47]. Behaviourally anchored scales [48] and frame-of-reference training [49] are classic ways to align raters, and assessor inconsistency has been documented in vocational qualifications [50].

Human-AI decision support. Guidelines for human-AI interaction stress showing why the system did what it did and making it easy to correct [51]. Automation bias, where people over-accept automated suggestions, is a known risk [52] and motivates both our explanations and our audit of overrides. Interpretability should be evaluated against the task it supports [53].

Audit and offline operation. Tamper-evident logging with hash chains dates back to Schneier and Kelsey [54], with efficient structures later proposed by Crosby and Wallach [55]. Local-first software argues for keeping data usable on the device without a connection [56]. Whisper offers multilingual speech recognition for a later voice-declaration feature [57].

3. Problem definition

Input. A worker w gives a free-text declaration d in English, Hindi or Hinglish. An assessor observes practical tasks and records a checklist, and may capture consented photos.

Outputs.

  1. A ranked list of qualification packs P from the set {target, distractors}, with, for every top pack, the declaration sentences matched to each performance criterion (PC) and the core PCs with no match (skill gaps).
  2. For every rubric criterion c, a suggested level ŝ(c) ∈ {0, 1, 2, 3, none}, a confidence and plain-language reasons.
  3. A final level s(c) per criterion chosen by the assessor, NOS-level and overall scores, and a recommendation for the assessor’s sign-off.

Constraints. No automatic certification; overrides need reasons; tamper-evident records; faces blurred before storage; works offline; runs on CPU; the vision model reports presence only.

Evaluation questions. Q1. How accurately does the mapper rank the correct pack (top-1, top-3, MRR), and is it equally good in all three languages? Q2. How reliably does perceptual hashing flag reused evidence without false alarms? Q3. How well does the detector find PPE (mAP50, mAP50-95)? Q4. Do assessors agree more with the rubric and pre scores than without them?

4. Methodology

4.1 Qualification data

We downloaded the official qualification files of five NQR qualifications (Table 1), recorded the URL, date and SHA-256 of each, and parsed the Module Summary and Detailed Assessment Criteria tables with pdfplumber. The NOS name sits in a vertically merged cell, so its text can appear anywhere inside a NOS block, even on the next page. The parser therefore groups rows into blocks at “Total” rows or PC-number restarts and assigns NOS codes by matching title tokens against the module summary with an optimal assignment. Quirks of the official files (a NOS table printed twice, a row repeated across a page break, two inconsistent NOS codes) are handled and documented. The parsed criteria have been spot-checked by us against the PDFs; a complete human check is still pending.

4.2 M1: qualification-pack mapper

The declaration is split into sentences (and long run-on sentences into clauses). A similarity function gives a matrix between declaration units and all 344 performance criteria. The aggregation was fixed before any evaluation: the score of a pack is the mean, over declaration units, of the best similarity to any core PC of that pack. A declaration is partial, so we measure how well a pack explains what the worker said, not how much of the pack they mentioned. For explanations, each PC gets the best similarity of any unit; a PC is “matched” above a threshold, and unmatched core PCs are reported as gaps.

Similarity functions: keyword (binary bag-of-words cosine after stop-word removal and light stemming), TF-IDF (unigrams and bigrams, IDF fitted on all PCs) [23], MiniLM (paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2, the pre-declared primary model) [14], [16], and LaBSE [15]. After seeing the first results we added an exploratory hybrid (mean of TF-IDF and MiniLM similarities); it is reported separately because it was designed after looking at the data.

Evaluation data. The team has not yet written the planned 150 real declarations. To test the pipeline we generated a synthetic set of 150 declarations (10 per pack and language) with an AI model that saw only the job titles and one-line job summaries, never the performance criteria, so it could not copy official wording. 16 % are “hard” cases that mention neighbouring trades. A labelling tool and guidelines for the real set are in the repository (docs/labelling_guidelines.md).

Metrics. Top-1 accuracy, top-3 accuracy and mean reciprocal rank [25], overall and per language, with 95 % bootstrap confidence intervals over declarations [46]. Ties (for example all-zero scores from a lexical baseline on Devanagari text) are scored as the expected value under a random order of the tied packs, so a baseline cannot gain credit from the order in which packs are listed.

4.3 M3: rubrics and pre scores

Six practical tasks for the Assistant Electrician qualification were written as YAML rubrics: (1) PPE, isolation and test before touch; (2) single-phase switch-board wiring; (3) conduit laying; (4) straight-through joint and insulation; (5) continuity and insulation testing with fault finding; (6) a small distribution board with MCBs. Each criterion lists the official NOS and PCs it covers (validated automatically against the parsed pack), a weight, a critical (safety) flag, the checklist items an assessor observes and four anchored level descriptors in the spirit of behaviourally anchored scales [48]. Hindi translations are provided for titles, instructions and checklist items. The level descriptors were drafted by the team with AI assistance and must be reviewed by a certified assessor before real use.

Pre-score rules are deliberately simple and shown to the assessor:

  • checklist items observed by the assessor drive the level: none ticked gives 0, some give 1, all give 2;
  • the engine never suggests 3 (proficient), which is left to the assessor’s judgement;
  • photo detections are reported as reasons (“gloves detected in photo p1, confidence 0.91”). With no checklist they can suggest at most level 2 for presence-type criteria at low confidence, and a disagreement with the checklist is flagged as a conflict;
  • the self-declaration is shown as context only and never raises a level;
  • with no observed evidence the engine makes no suggestion.

Aggregation: criterion score = level / 3; NOS and overall scores are weighted means; the default pass mark is the official 70 % aggregate stated in the Assistant Electrician qualification file; any critical criterion below level 2 blocks a positive recommendation. All of this is covered by unit tests, including the edge cases (all zeros, exactly at the pass mark, missing scores, invalid levels, weights).

4.4 M2: evidence checker

Privacy. Consent must be confirmed before a photo is accepted. Faces are detected with OpenCV’s Haar cascades [58] and blurred before anything is written to disk.

Reuse detection. For every stored photo we compute pHash and dHash [35], [39]. A new photo whose pHash is within a Hamming threshold of a photo from another worker’s assessment is flagged for the assessor; nothing is rejected automatically. We evaluated four hashes on the test split of a public PPE dataset (CC BY 4.0). Because that split turned out to be dominated by near-identical video frames, we first grouped near-identical images with an independent measure (32×32 thumbnail correlation), kept one per group, and drew stored (“gallery”) and fresh photos from different groups. Each gallery photo was re-submitted after one of nine transformations (recompression, resizing, cropping, brightness, rotation, a messaging-app style border, mirroring). The threshold was chosen on a development half as the highest recall with at most 5 % false flags on fresh photos, and is reported on the held-out half.

Presence detector. A YOLOv8n model [29], [30] is fine-tuned on Colab on the same public PPE dataset (classes mapped to helmet, gloves, safety shoes, goggles), optionally with the team’s own labelled photos of electrician tools. The notebook measures train/test leakage from near-duplicate frames and reports mAP on a clean test subset. CPU inference is wired into the application. The detector reports presence only and does not judge workmanship.

4.5 M4: consistency study protocol

To answer Q4 we will run a within-subject study with 3 to 5 real assessors (not members of the team who wrote the rubrics) on 20 to 30 consented evidence items. Each assessor scores every item twice: first unassisted (own judgement, level names only) and, after a gap of at least one week, assisted (anchored descriptors and the tool’s pre score with reasons). Item order is randomised per rater and condition but reproducible. Agreement is measured per condition with Fleiss’ kappa [41], ordinal Krippendorff’s alpha [43], [44] and pairwise exact and within-one agreement, with bootstrap CIs over items [46], and the assisted-minus-unassisted difference in alpha is bootstrapped over items rated in both conditions. The analysis code is tested on simulated data inside the test suite only; no simulated result is reported.

4.6 Where the tool supports and where it does not replace human judgement

5. System design

Hunar is a FastAPI backend with SQLite (via SQLModel) and a React progressive web app (Figure 1). The ML modules sit behind service interfaces, so models can be swapped without touching the API.

Device (PWA, offline)                       Server (CPU laptop)
 React screens EN/HI ──► Dexie outbox ──► /api/sync ──► services ──► M1 mapper (sentence-transformers)
        │                (ordered, idempotent)        │          ├─► M2 face blur, pHash, YOLOv8n
        └──────── GET when online ───────────────────►│          ├─► M3 rubric engine (YAML)
                                                       │          └─► audit log (SHA-256 chain)
                                                       └──► SQLite, blurred photos on disk

Figure 1. Components. Mermaid diagrams are in docs/architecture.md.

Offline operation. Every write goes through a durable IndexedDB outbox. Records created offline get client-generated UUIDs, so later operations can refer to them before they reach the server. The sync endpoint applies operations in order, records each operation id so a replay is ignored, and stops at the first rejected operation so dependent operations are not applied out of order. Mapping needs the server, so a declaration typed offline is matched after sync.

Audit trail. Each audit entry stores the SHA-256 of the previous entry; editing any past entry breaks the chain, which /api/audit/verify detects, and SQLite triggers refuse UPDATE and DELETE on the table. A test shows that even an attacker who drops the trigger and edits an entry is detected.

Interfaces. Screens: worker/facilitator declaration with explained mapping; practical tasks with checklist and photo capture; assessor scoring with anchors, pre scores, reasons and accept/override; competency profile and sign-off; admin audit view with chain verification; rater mode for the study. Sample data is badged “SAMPLE DATA” everywhere.

6.1 Discussion

Q1, mapper. On the synthetic set the lexical baselines do well in English and Hinglish, where the workers’ words overlap with the English criteria, but fall to near chance in Devanagari Hindi: TF-IDF top-1 is 0.25 against a chance level of 0.20. The multilingual model is the only single method that works across all three languages (Hindi top-3 0.88 against 0.62 for TF-IDF), which is the fairness argument for using it. It is weaker than TF-IDF on Hinglish (0.46 against 0.64), probably because romanised Hindi was rare in its training data. LaBSE did not help. The exploratory hybrid combines the strengths of both (overall top-1 0.59, top-3 0.92), but it was designed after seeing these results and must be confirmed on the real team-written set. The confusion matrix shows the most common error: declarations are pulled towards Industrial Electrician, a large qualification whose criteria cover broad wiring and testing vocabulary. The confidence intervals are wide (n = 50 per language), and the synthetic declarations were written by a model rather than by workers, so these numbers say that the pipeline works and where it is weak, not how it will perform in the field.

Q2, reuse detection. At an operating point with very few false alarms, pHash reliably catches re-submission of the same photo after recompression or resizing, which is the most likely form of casual reuse (for example a photo forwarded through a messaging app). It does not catch deliberate edits such as cropping, rotating or adding borders; raising the threshold to catch them would flag a large share of genuinely different site photos, because construction-site photos look alike. The public dataset itself was mostly near-identical video frames, a reminder that the same kind of leakage could inflate detector scores if not controlled.

Q3, detector and Q4, assessor agreement are pending (Section 8).

7. Limitations

  • No real worker data yet. M1 results come from synthetic declarations written by an AI model; real performance will differ and is likely lower. The team-written set is the next step.
  • Single trade, five qualifications. Distractors are neighbouring electrical qualifications; with hundreds of packs on the NQR the task becomes harder.
  • Parsing not fully human-checked. Performance criteria were spot-checked, not checked one by one.
  • Rubric descriptors are drafts. They were drafted by the team with AI help from official performance criteria and need review by a certified assessor or the sector skill council.
  • Presence, not quality. The detector, once trained, can only say that gloves or a multimeter are visible. It cannot tell whether a joint is sound.
  • Face blurring uses Haar cascades, which miss profile, small and occluded faces. Photos taken offline wait unblurred in the device’s queue until sync.
  • Hashing misses edited reuse (crops, rotations, borders, mirroring).
  • Assessor agreement is unmeasured. This is the outcome the problem statement cares most about, and it requires real assessors and consented photos.
  • Theory component. NSQF assessment also includes theory and viva marks; Hunar supports only the practical component.

8. Ethics, consent and responsible use

  • Consent. A consent form template (docs/consent_form.md) covers the purposes, blurring, storage, withdrawal and deletion. The application refuses a photo until consent is confirmed.
  • Privacy. Workers are identified only by pseudonymous codes; faces are blurred before storage; raw data and the database are excluded from version control.
  • Human oversight. The tool cannot certify anyone. The assessor confirms the pack, decides every criterion, and signs off; disagreeing with the tool is a first-class action. Overrides are logged with reasons, which also makes automation bias [52] auditable: a high rate of unexamined acceptance would be visible in the log.
  • Fairness. Accuracy is reported per language; a model that only worked in English would be excluded on fairness grounds.
  • Honest limits. The interface states that the photo model judges presence only.

  4. Run the assessor-agreement study with 3 to 5 assessors.
  5. Add spoken declarations with Whisper-based speech recognition [57] and on-device face blurring.
  6. Extend to more trades by running the same parser on more NQR qualification files.
